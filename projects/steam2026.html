Steam genres, six years later

Before moving to the meat of the article, here is the mandatory disclaimer about AI usage (we live in strange times). During the production of this article I used the AI model Claude to write the SQL queries (which I ran on my own database), suggest avenues to explore, fact-check my drafts against the data, suggest edits and draft some passages, and prepare the chart data and the documentation of the dataset. I personally verified everything it produced, and the entire article was peer reviewed by people I know before publication. Everything the analysis relies on, from the raw data to the queries, is freely available at the end of the article, so you can check it yourself.

On 12 September 2026, Valve’s digital store, Steam, celebrated 23 years of activity. It so happens that in 2020 I attempted to predict the following three years of the platform’s bustling activity. Today I will look back at what I stated then and maybe uncover some interesting findings.

I originally analysed over 40,000 games, or 69,000 counting DLCs. Before assessing my past work, I re-fetched every app, plus all the new ones released since, bringing the total to about 113,000 games, or 169,000 with DLCs (excluding 2026, which is still in progress). Nearly two in three of those games came out between 2021 and 2025. While 2020 saw 9,242 new games hit the store, 2025 saw 21,098, more than double. Surprisingly, the average price of a paid game barely moved, from €8.40 in 2020 to €8.44 in 2025, which makes it about 18% cheaper after inflation.

The new data was collected through Steam’s public store API from August to October 2026. Every year up to 2025 is included in full, excluding games not yet released or removed from the store. I looked at the same ten genres as in 2020; a game counts in every genre it carries, so genre shares add up to more than 100%. About 6% of the games in my 2020 data can no longer be bought, though only the 2% removed from the store entirely are left out of the counts.

All prices are in euros and refer to the list price, before any discount. Genre and yearly averages cover only paid games. Where noted, prices are adjusted for inflation using the euro area consumer price index from Eurostat, which rose 22.6% from 2020 to 2025. 2020 prices come from the data collected for the previous article, and reproduce its figures exactly; games from 2021 onward show today’s prices. I chose to treat every price as if it were the game’s launch price, because the same games released in 2020 cost on average only 3% less today, and half of them never changed price.

Checking the 2020 article

Releases

As in 2020, games come in a great variety of genres. Action, Adventure and Casual remain the kings (leaving aside Indie, which appears on most games): together they are still on more than 80% of releases. But looking at each genre individually, it turns out my prediction of +30–45% underestimated the growth. Compared with the full year 2020, Racing and RPG games grew by almost 60% by 2023, and Adventure, Simulation and Casual by more than 45%. Strategy (43%) and Action (38%) landed inside the range, while Indie (30%) and Sports (23%) fell just below it. MMO grew the least, 16%, close to the 13% I had predicted.

Prices

When I started querying the database to see how much a game cost, something odd popped up. The average price of a Sports game had jumped from €11.56 in 2020 to €25.74 in 2023. I had to dig deeper. It turns out that from 2021 onwards a few publishers listed hundreds of small games at €194.99, spread across almost every genre. Some of the same publishers’ older games were raised too: titles that sold for €8.19 in 2020 are now listed at €194.99, with a permanent-looking 95% discount that brings them back to €9.74. That’s why every game listed at €190 or more is left out of the price averages that follow. Ironically, those listings would have proven me right: in 2020 I predicted the average Sports game would pass €19 by 2023. Without them, it actually fell to €10.59.

DLCs had a similar outlier. In 2021 alone, one publisher released 1,996 DLCs for just 20 games, about 100 each. That publisher is left out of the DLC figures.

Talking about pricing predictions, I can safely say I missed almost all of them. I expected most averages to rise by 25–30% by 2023, and only MMOs to contract, by 5.7% to just over €9. In the end, the highest increase was Casual’s 18.2%, followed by Adventure’s 12.0%. Everything else sits between −13.1% and +5.5%. The hardest hit was Racing, which I expected to grow by 57%: its average price fell from €9.83 to €8.54. Sports lost its crown as the most expensive genre, stopping at €10.59, a few cents behind RPG’s €10.63, before regaining it in 2025. And MMOs, the one genre I got roughly right, fell by 3.1% to €9.33.

One could think this was caused by more free-to-play games, but their share of new games barely changed: about 15% of the games released in 2020 and 16.6% of those released in 2023. And Racing and Sports, the two genres whose prices fell the most, had the lowest free share in 2023, about 12%. Free-to-play can’t explain the fall. Prices simply barely moved, which, once inflation is taken into account, means they fell. More on that later.

DLCs

A game needs content, and in recent years Steam has seen both the basic offer and the additional content getting bigger and bigger. Back in 2020 I highlighted a trend that was engulfing a few genres, especially MMOs, that DLCs would see fewer releases than the number of games. I suggested that, if the trend continued, MMO DLCs could disappear entirely by 2023. It didn’t happen. The decline continued until 2021, when 228 new MMO games were released against just 118 new DLCs, 58% fewer than the peak of 281 in 2018. Then it reversed: 213 in 2023, 424 in 2024 and a record 472 in 2025, once again outnumbering MMO games. Other genres saw the same resurgence, and all but Sports set a new record in 2025: Action 2,758, Indie 3,692, RPG 2,691, Simulation 2,439.

Looking back, what I tried to predict in 2020 split neatly in two. Where I described broad patterns, I was mostly right: every genre kept growing, Action, Adventure and Casual stayed on top, and MMOs remained the slowest. Where I extended the last couple of years into the future, I was mostly wrong. Prices didn’t keep climbing, and MMO DLCs didn’t fade away; if anything, they came back stronger. The market simply didn’t follow the trends of 2018–2020. So what did it do instead? That’s what the rest of this article looks at.

What changed since 2020

Now that we know what I expected 2023 to look like, we can dive into what it actually turned out to be, and look further, to 2025.

As I said, prices barely moved between 2020 and 2025, but the same can’t be said for the number of new games. 17,712 hit the shelves in 2024, almost double 2020, and 2025 set a new record with 21,098. Simulation games grew by 81% between 2023 and 2025, and every other genre by 25–70%.

All these new titles didn’t change what games cost, but they did change where prices sit. If we split every paid game into four arbitrary ranges (under €5, €5–9.99, €10–14.99, and €15 and up), the middle range grew from about 24% of games in 2020 to 28% in 2025. In genres like Casual and RPG, fewer games launched at the cheapest prices and more in the middle, while in Simulation and Strategy the same happened at the top end: fewer games launched at €15 and up.

2024 was also an unusually big year for free games, which grew faster than paid ones: 3,106 entered the store, 922 more than in 2023, a 42% jump. In 2025 the story went the other way. The number of free games stayed flat while paid ones kept growing, so free games fell back to 14.3% of new releases, about where they were in 2020.

If we zoom further out and look at the big picture, a few genres show strengthening trends over the last few years. While games can carry multiple genres, those carrying Casual rose from 26.5% of all 2014 releases to 44.0% in 2020 and 46.8% in 2025, consolidating their place as the second most common genre in the store, right after the considerably larger Indie (71.2% in 2025). Meanwhile Action, after hovering between 40% and 44% for most of the decade, has been slipping since 2023, ending for now at 36.8% in 2025. Its battle with Adventure has tipped too: after running neck and neck from 2021 to 2023, Adventure pulled clearly ahead in 2024 and 2025, though it also lost share along the way. The most interesting change may instead be the steady rise of Simulation games, whose share has grown every year since 2021, reaching a record 24.2% in 2025.

Of all these games the majority are paid, and as I said before, their average price didn’t keep up with inflation. After cutting off at €190, the remaining games show an average that has only moved by €0.04 (0.5%) across five years, while inflation in the euro area rose 22.6% in the same period. A game at €9.99 in 2020 would need to cost €12.24 in 2025 just to stand still.

As always, not every genre is built the same. Of all genres, Strategy got hit the hardest, with its 2025 games showing a fall of 24.8% in real price once inflation is applied. Simulation’s 2025 average fell to €9.09, which earns it second place with a loss of 24.6%, and Racing followed closely at 23.1%. Every other genre fell by 6–19%, with Casual, at 6.5%, the only one that came close to keeping pace. As for the games released in 2020, they cost on average 3% less today than at launch, and half have never changed their price, which indicates that once a game’s price is set, it rarely gets updated with inflation. Many of the changes that did happen are consistent with Valve’s October 2022 update of its recommended prices: €9.75, €19.50 and €24.50, unused in 2020, are now on 6.9% of paid apps, while €8.19 and €16.79 fell from 6.7% to 2.5%.

Talking about games is only half the story, as DLCs exist too. If we look at their averages, a DLC in 2020 cost €8.05, slightly less than a game’s €8.40, but by 2025 it had risen 18.5% to €9.54, now more than a game’s €8.44. After inflation that leaves a loss of only 3.3% (looking at medians instead, it is even positive, +2.0%). Strategy shows the opposite of its games here: while they were the hardest hit, its DLCs had the biggest rise, beating inflation by 17.1%. Casual is the other side of the coin, the only genre whose DLCs fared worse than its games, losing 20.8% against 6.5%. In the end, in five of the nine genres, DLCs beat inflation.

Steam’s importance for the video game industry has only grown since the first non-Valve title reached its digital shelves. With more new releases than ever before, it is now a more crowded place for new and seasoned developers to keep pushing the boundaries of what a game can be. And yet, games have not become more expensive; after inflation, they have even become cheaper. If the last five years and the five before are any guide, we can expect more surprises.

Data and notes

Data. Both datasets, the one behind the 2020 article and the new one, are available here.

Inflation. Prices after inflation are in 2025 euros, using Eurostat’s annual harmonised index of consumer prices for the euro area (20 countries, table tec00027).

Excluded listings. Every game or DLC listed at €190 or more is left out of the price averages. Two publishers are also left out of all price figures from 2021 onward, together with their games’ DLCs: the one behind most of the €194.99 listings, and the one behind the 2021 wave of DLCs, which is left out of the DLC counts too.

Price ranges. The ranges use list prices, not prices after inflation. Steam prices sit just below round numbers (€4.99, €9.99), right where the edges of the ranges fall, so adjusting older prices would push many of them into the next range and fake a shift towards pricier games. The comparison also skips 2021 and 2022, whose games have had the longest time to see their price cut.

DLCs. A DLC counts in the genres it carries itself, which aren’t always those of its game. Soundtracks count as music, not DLCs, even where Steam listed them as DLCs in 2020.

Free games and survivorship. Whether a game is free comes from its store page today, so free games that have shut down drop out of the count, and the free share of older release years is lower than it was at the time. It isn’t a small effect: at least 552 games that were free in my 2020 data have since shut down, 83 of them removed from the store entirely. MMOs are hit hardest, with at least 229 gone, about a third of the free MMOs I counted in 2020. That’s why I only compare free shares from 2020 onward, and even those of the earliest years are probably a little low.

Sources. Valve’s update of its recommended regional prices, 25 October 2022, and SteamDB’s list of the new prices.
